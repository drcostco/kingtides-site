    Sources and method

    How the numbers are made

    The tide table is NOAA's astronomical prediction. The water runs above or below it because of ocean temperature, pressure, wind and river flow; that gap at high water is the skew surge. For each coming high tide, four estimates of the skew surge are combined into one probability distribution: the last three tides carried forward, with error bars taken from 47 years of gauge history at the same lead time; the five-day baseline adjusted for forecast pressure, with the slope fitted on the last month; NOAA's San Francisco Bay model; and NOAA's global surge model, each corrected for its recent error at this gauge. Sources that disagree widen the range rather than being averaged away.

    A flood spot's threshold is the gauge level at which it has been observed flooded or closed. The chance of water is the chance the forecast level exceeds that threshold, including the threshold's own uncertainty. A spot is treated as blocked until crews have typically reopened it. Where the record shows rain lowers the level at which a spot floods, the threshold is lowered when the models show rain before that tide; that adjustment is a judgment, not a fitted relationship.

    Limits worth knowing: drive times have no traffic in them; flood spots only cover the corridors of the listed trips; thresholds for the freeway main lanes rest on one event (January 3, 2026) plus the days it did not flood; and nothing here sees a levee failure coming.
